## Known open issues / lessons learned so far

- In this non-minimum-phase regime the pumps oscillate anti-phase (v1 high while v2 low), so a 50-sample block can show a large split excursion while the total-effort excess stays near zero; requiring the split excess to be a fixed multiple of the total excess (ratio band) correctly rejects such healthy control-interaction swings that a standalone dS threshold would false-flag.
- In this plant the faulted pump is already near its 12 V rail in healthy operation (v1 mean 10.42 V, only ~1.6 V headroom), so any detector whose gate requires the SUM of both pump efforts to rise by a fixed absolute amount (e.g. dT>2.8 V) is physically unreachable for a single-tank leak and reports zero detections regardless of fault severity; the effort signal that actually moves is the anti-phase SHIFT (v1-v2), not the total.
- Because each controlled tank is driven by its OWN directly-actuated pump (v1->h1, v2->h2), a cross-coupled fault perturbs the healthy tank but its fast local loop re-regulates its level, so the healthy tank's sustained |error| stays near the measured healthy mean while only the saturated/faulted tank sustains large error; therefore per-tank sustained block-mean |error| is a cross-coupling-robust discriminator that effort-only logic cannot provide.
- A detector whose persistence test uses the full 50-sample window keeps asserting a fault for up to a full window (or half-window) after the fault clears, producing exactly the observed ~50 post-clear false-positive events per fault scenario; shortening the recent decision window to ~20 samples reduces this lag-driven false-positive count without materially hurting detection.
- A per-tank anomaly bar scaled off the healthy MEAN |error| (e.g. 2x the 0.074 mean = 0.15) sits INSIDE the healthy envelope because the healthy MAX |error| is 0.19, so slow control-loop oscillation peaks false-positive; the bar must instead be placed strictly above the measured healthy MAXIMUM |error| (here 1.05-1.08x 0.19), which makes a fault-free crossing mathematically impossible for any block average and any persistence fraction, guaranteeing zero baseline flags without relying on noise averaging.
- Because the supervisor's own mitigation for a tank in deficit (lowering its setpoint toward the achievable level) shrinks the very tracking-error signal used as the anomaly evidence, an error-magnitude detector can un-detect an ongoing fault once the setpoint has been driven further below nominal than the detection margin, producing a mitigate -> release -> re-flag chatter whose released steps are counted as missed anomalies; the per-step mitigation amplitude must therefore be bounded well inside the detection margin (here 0.005 V/step against a ~0.015 V margin between the healthy envelope maximum and the detection bar).
- When a detector's only tight fault-free statistic is a bounded quantity (here |error| with a measured maximum), the decision bar is NOT the sensitivity limiter: any mean or fraction of samples can never exceed the maximum of those samples, so the bar can be set arbitrarily close to the measured max with zero in-sample baseline false positives. The real limiter of both missed anomalies (onset latency) and post-clear false positives (release latency) is the decision-window length, so sensitivity should be bought by shortening the recent block, not by raising the bar.
- Equal missed_anomaly and false_positive counts on the same tank within a single-fault scenario (e.g. 51 and 51) are the signature of a phase-lagged detector (late to engage and late to release), not of a mis-placed threshold: a wrong bar gives asymmetric counts (either mostly missed or mostly false), whereas a symmetric lag gives exactly this pair, so the fix is a shorter/split decision window rather than moving the bar.
- For a plant that is already actuator-saturated in healthy operation, the supervisor's own setpoint mitigation shrinks the level-based evidence by roughly the same amount as the setpoint move; therefore the mitigation's TOTAL excursion (rate times cap), not just its per-step rate, must be bounded inside the gap between the healthy maximum and the detection bar, otherwise a persistent fault produces mitigate -> release -> re-flag chatter whose released steps are logged as missed anomalies.
- A block-mean-of-|error| test and a fraction-of-samples-above-the-bar test are not equivalent ways to buy sensitivity: a per-tank bar placed a mere 1-2% above the measured healthy maximum is provably unreachable by the block MEAN (a mean can never exceed the block maximum, and with a 16-sample block the standard error of the mean puts even that at ~10 sigma), whereas the FRACTION test additionally needs enough samples above the bar and is the weaker of the two; therefore a short-block MEAN test is the cheapest reliable detector once a measured healthy maximum exists, and it should be preferred over fraction/persistence games that only add release lag.
- When a fault signal is a bounded quantity (|error|) whose healthy maximum is fixed, the observable miss/false trade-off lives entirely in the decision-window LENGTH and not in the bar height, because any mean or fraction of a block is bounded by that block's maximum regardless of how the bar moves. Shrinking the recent decision window from the 24-sample neighborhood to 16 samples of a 50-sample buffer reduces both the onset latency and the post-clear release latency by the same amount, which removes the symmetric missed/false pairs (51/51) that a longer block produces.
- Pump-effort saturation can still be used as a detection channel in an actuator-saturated plant, but only as a SECOND route that requires the saturation to be sustained over the averaging block (e.g. a 16 s mean at or above 99% of the supply rail) AND simultaneous tracking error at the healthy maximum; single-sample rail hits are a healthy anti-phase artifact and averaging over >=16 samples separates the sustained-saturation regime from the healthy spike regime without any absolute rail-proximity threshold.
- A symmetric missed/false pair (51/51) survives on both leak scenarios even though the left-over persistence block was only 24 samples long, which bounds how much of the pair can be blamed on decision-window lag: the residual ~50 samples in each direction is the plant's own |error| rise time above the detection bar after fault onset and its decay time below the bar after the fault clears (settling 100-300 s), so for a slow plant a pure magnitude-threshold detector has a floor on missed+false that no amount of window shortening can go below - further gains require a physically faster observable, not a shorter block.
- When a fault-free quantity has a hard measured ceiling (here healthy |error| max 0.19), adding a per-sample count test (K of the last W samples above the bar) to the block-mean test at the SAME bar is strictly free: in the healthy record zero samples ever reach the bar, so K can never be accumulated no matter how the window is chosen, while for a fault whose error oscillates around the bar the count test engages as soon as K samples cross - earlier than a block mean, which additionally requires the average of the whole block to cross - so the count route lowers onset latency without costing any baseline margin.
- Once a per-sample count test at a bar above the healthy ceiling exists, keeping a block-MEAN route ORed alongside it is pure downside: the mean route can never trip earlier than the count route at the same bar (a block mean cannot exceed a bar before one of its samples does), but it holds the flag True for as long as its longer averaging window still contains pre-clear samples, so it silently re-extends the post-clear false-positive tail to the longest window in the OR; the mean/fraction routes should be deleted, not kept as a safety net.
- Calibrating the detection bar from a single measured healthy episode leaves residual uncertainty about the true healthy maximum on a held-out episode, and a count requirement of K=2 (instead of K=1) inside a short window removes vulnerability to a single-sample outlier at a cost of only one sample of onset latency for a rising fault; K=2 is therefore the better default when the bar is derived from finite data, whereas K=1 is only optimal when the healthy ceiling is known exactly.
- In a battery scored as IAE + 500*violations + 300*missed + FP_PENALTY*false, a single severe-persistent tank can dominate everything: 171 safety-violation steps (85500 points of a 100941-point scenario) while its IAE is only 140.8, and the violation count is essentially invariant (171, 171, 169) across three structurally different supervisory policies, which shows that once the faulted tank's own pump is pinned at the supply rail (here v1's healthy mean is 10.42 V against a 12 V rail) the tank level is plant-determined and no setpoint command in the supervisor's repertoire can move it, so that scenario's score is immovable and detector tuning should be judged only on the missed/false terms it can actually influence.
- Simultaneously active faults break per-tank calibration derived from single-fault episodes: the same tank records roughly twice the missed-anomaly steps in the two-fault episode as in its own single-fault episode (tank2: 101 vs 51), because the cross-coupled inflow from the other tank's fault shifts the second tank in the direction that slows its |error| rise, so any fixed |error| bar is crossed later and the onset latency grows with the number of concurrent faults.
- A count-of-K-in-W persistence rule is not merely a faster block mean: after a fault clears it yields a pulsed flag that is re-asserted only while residual error peaks still cross the bar, so the post-clear false-positive count is bounded by the number of above-bar samples during the decay rather than by the full decay time, whereas a fraction-of-samples-above-bar rule keeps the flag asserted continuously for roughly (1-fraction)*W samples after the last crossing and therefore logs the entire decay tail as contiguous false positives.
- The healthy fault-free error envelope of this plant is strongly ASYMMETRIC (tank1 [-0.030, +0.190], tank2 [-0.087, +0.191]), so a detector written as '|error| > 1.05 x max|error|' silently makes a draining fault travel the LOOSE-edge distance (0.19) even though the tight edge is only 0.03-0.09 away; placing an extra per-sample bar on the tight edge, derived from the same measured envelope and displaced by an equal ABSOLUTE margin, cuts onset latency by roughly 3x for tank1 at identical baseline safety, whereas a bar placed on the tight edge with a purely multiplicative (1.06x) margin is too thin in absolute terms (0.002) and is not robust to held-out healthy variation.
- A stateless supervisor can recover the sign convention of a telemetry 'error' field from the window itself by comparing sum|dev - error| with sum|dev + error|, where dev = level - target: the two hypotheses differ by ~2*|dev| (about 0.12 here) while the correct one has a residual of order 1e-9 with an exact setpoint, so the convention is identified with ~1e2 margin and a wrong choice is essentially impossible; this matters because testing the tight envelope edge in the WRONG convention puts the bar INSIDE the healthy envelope and floods the baseline with false positives, so the fallback for any ambiguous case (ratio >= 0.15) must be the wide mirrored bars, never the tight ones.
- In this fault-free record the error oscillation already reaches the LOW edge of its healthy envelope repeatedly within every cycle, while the HIGH edge is reached only at the oscillation peaks, so a detection bar placed just outside the LOW edge is crossed almost as soon as a leak perturbs the loop, whereas a bar placed on |error| at the far HIGH edge must wait for the whole envelope to travel downward; the detection latency of a magnitude threshold is therefore set by the distance from the FAULT-DIRECTED envelope edge, and window shortening cannot buy back a badly placed edge.
- A one-sided magnitude bar is not 'free' even when provably outside the healthy envelope: moving it tighter by d cuts onset latency by d/(fault rate) but lengthens the post-clear release by the same d/(recovery rate), so the score-optimal tightness is fixed by the ratio of the missed-anomaly penalty to the false-positive penalty (here 300 vs 160-250), and with the miss penalty above the FP penalty the best bar is the tightest one that still clears the healthy envelope - latency minimisation alone is the wrong objective.
- Under a non-minimum-phase pump/valve split, a tank's own-fault error excursion and the cross-coupled disturbance it imposes on its neighbour have OPPOSITE signs (own leak -> level falls -> tight/low edge; neighbour's leak -> extra cross inflow -> level rises -> wide/high edge), so one one-sided bar on the fault-directed edge obtains the onset speed of a tight threshold together with the baseline safety of a wide one - something no symmetric |error| threshold can achieve, since it must pay the wide margin on both sides.
- For a supervisor whose diagnosis channel IS the tracking error, setpoint trimming is score-neutral to negative on a leak battery: the faulted pump is already pinned at the rail, so trimming the reference buys at most (trim amplitude x fault duration) in IAE - here order 1 point against 1e4-1e5 points of miss/false terms - while it directly shifts the error used as evidence (mitigate -> release -> re-flag chatter) and leaves a non-zero restore_gap if the fault has not cleared by episode end; holding and restoring the nominal reference is therefore strictly safer.
