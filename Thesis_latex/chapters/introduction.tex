\section{Motivation}
Continuous processes in mineral processing run around the clock and must
deliver a product of consistent quality while respecting equipment and
safety constraints. They are strongly nonlinear and tightly coupled, several
key process states are only indirectly measured, and the control solutions
must be transparent enough for operators and engineers to trust and
maintain \cite{pomerleau2000}.

A classic example is the control of a grinding mill circuit, in which the
mill, the sump and the classification unit (cyclone clusters) form a coupled
process that determines the product particle size and the operating
conditions of the circuit. The objective is to regulate the product P80,
the particle size below which 80\% of the product mass falls, around a
target while keeping the sump level stable and the circulating load within
its limits. Variations in fresh feed flow, ore density and percentage of
solids change the operating conditions, so an action that improves particle
size regulation can upset the sump level or the circulating load. The
supervisory controller must coordinate setpoint changes to satisfy these
competing requirements and maintain performance under conditions beyond
those used during its development.

In practice, grinding circuits use PID loops whose setpoints are adjusted by
operators, expert systems or fuzzy-logic supervisors. These build on
established operating knowledge but require considerable tuning, and
hand-crafted rules grow complex as new conditions are added. Model
predictive control and, more recently, learning-based controllers that
combine learned world models with planning, such as TD-MPC
\cite{hansen2022tdmpc,hansen2024tdmpc2}, coordinate the loops through
prediction and optimization, but their decisions are hard to inspect and
their behaviour beyond the training data is uncertain. This leaves a gap
between supervisory logic that is interpretable but costly to maintain and
controllers that are adaptive but opaque.

Large language models (LLMs) can generate and iteratively refine code and
heuristics from environment feedback
\cite{romeraparedes2024funsearch,novikov2025alphaevolve,ma2024eureka,hatgiskessell2026promptpo,weng2026lbg}.
Using an LLM directly in the control loop is unsuitable because of latency,
non-determinism and safety risks. This thesis therefore uses the LLM as an
offline \emph{meta-supervisor} that synthesizes explicit, interpretable
supervisory rules. The rules run locally and update the setpoints of the
existing low-level controllers, and they are benchmarked against Weir's
current TD-MPC-type recommendation algorithm, which combines learned
world-model prediction with control.

\section{Research questions and hypotheses}
The objective is to evaluate how well LLM-synthesized supervisory heuristics
coordinate setpoints in a grinding circuit compared with a model-based
predictive supervisory controller, and to identify where their performance
degrades.
\begin{description}
\item[RQ1 (Comparative performance)] How does an LLM-synthesized supervisory
  heuristic compare with a model-based predictive supervisory controller in
  mill circuit control, in terms of P80 regulation, sump level stability,
  compliance with circulating load limits and smoothness of setpoint
  adjustments, under nominal conditions and disturbances within the
  development range?
\item[RQ2 (Robustness and generalizability)] How does this performance
  change when the amplitudes, temporal patterns or combinations of
  disturbances fall outside the development range, and what failure modes
  and empirical operating limits can be identified?
\end{description}
The research questions are tested through the following null hypotheses:
\begin{description}
\item[$\boldsymbol{H_{0,1}}$:] Within the development range, there is no
  difference in performance metrics between the LLM-synthesized supervisor
  and the benchmark controller.
\item[$\boldsymbol{H_{0,2}}$:] Beyond the development range, the degradation
  in performance relative to the development range is the same for both
  controllers.
\end{description}

\section{Contributions}
The expected scientific contributions are:
\begin{description}
\item[C1 (RQ1)] A quantified comparison, with effect sizes and confidence
  intervals, of an LLM-synthesized supervisor and a TD-MPC controller on P80
  regulation, sump level, circulating load and setpoint smoothness, tested
  through $H_{0,1}$.
\item[C2 (RQ2)] Degradation curves showing how the performance of each
  controller changes as disturbances move beyond the development range,
  tested through $H_{0,2}$, together with a catalogue of observed failure
  modes and an empirical operating envelope for the LLM-based supervisor.
\item[C3] An estimate of how much performance varies between independently
  generated policies, indicating how reliable LLM-based synthesis is as a
  design method.
\end{description}
Before the industrial study, the method was developed on three toy processes
of increasing difficulty (Chapter~\ref{ch:toy}): a single tank, a two-tank
cascade and the quadruple-tank process. This preliminary work produced the
architecture, the security sandbox and the evaluation safeguards used in the
industrial study, and an ablation of how much LLM reasoning the synthesis
needs. A setpoint-coordination version of the quadruple-tank process then
rehearsed the comparison of RQ1 and RQ2 against a model predictive
controller (Chapter~\ref{ch:coord}).

\section{Delimitations}
The study is limited to one grinding circuit in simulation. Formal stability
guarantees, plant deployment and sensor-fault diagnosis are outside the
scope.
